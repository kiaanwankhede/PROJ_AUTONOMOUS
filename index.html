The Autonomous Enterprise

Earn autonomy one governed process at a time. “Autonomous enterprise” is mostly a vendor label for a 25-year-old idea, in which business processes run as governed sense–decide–act loops under authority that people can revoke. In November 2026 only about 17% of organizations have deployed AI agents at all. This briefing sets out what is real, the concepts the Gen AI team needs, and a 24-month plan that raises autonomy only on evidence.

The short answer

A label for an old idea

The phrase belongs mostly to SAP, which built Sapphire 2026 around it. The idea goes back to IBM’s autonomic computing in 2001. Every credible definition keeps people setting intent and accountable for outcomes.

Reality trails the spending

About 17% of organizations have deployed agents (Gartner), and 89% of executives in a central-bank survey report no productivity effect so far (NBER). Gartner expects 40% of enterprises to demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027.

Engineering discipline decides success

Earn autonomy one governed process at a time

The evidence behind the plan, with 223 linked sources. Figures are dated, and vendor-reported numbers are marked as such.

The "autonomous enterprise" is a marketing label, not an established discipline. No analyst firm or standards body publishes a canonical definition, and the exact phrase comes mainly from SAP, which built Sapphire in May 2026 around it, along with automation vendors and systems integrators. Strip away the branding and a defensible idea remains: an organization where a measured share of end-to-end processes runs as governed sense-decide-act loops under delegated, revocable authority, while humans set intent and stay accountable. By that standard almost no enterprise is autonomous in November 2026. Only about 17% of organizations have deployed AI agents at all (Gartner), agent deployment is in the single digits across nearly all business functions (Stanford AI Index 2026), and 89% of the nearly 6,000 executives in a central-bank survey report no AI effect on labor productivity over the past three years (NBER). Gartner, meanwhile, has raised its 2026 AI-spending forecast to about $2.7 trillion (CIO.com). Analysts now expect a correction: Gartner predicts 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 after production incidents (Gartner), and Forrester expects fewer than 10% of enterprises to use autonomous agents across applications in 2027 (Forrester). Two things are real today: narrow, verifiable automation (coding, IT service desks, document-heavy back-office work) and a fast-maturing control plane of open protocols, agent identity, evaluation and policy tooling. For a Gen AI development team, the skills that decide success are therefore less about models and more about context engineering, evaluation, least-privilege identity, design that resists prompt injection, cost per completed task and clear business ownership. The plan below treats autonomy as a dial set separately for each process. It runs in five phases over roughly 24 months: build the governed platform first, prove two or three reversible, measurable pilots in which humans approve consequential actions, and raise a process to bounded autonomy only when evals, incident data and unit economics justify it.

The term is SAP's 2026 slogan for a 25-year-old idea

Each camp in the market has its own word for roughly the same ambition, and the choice tracks what the speaker sells. Analysts with no platform to sell use neutral terms focused on outcomes. Gartner's umbrella concept is "autonomous business", defined as "a strategy that uses self-improving, adaptable technology to make decisions, take action and create new types of value by increasing both people autonomy and machine autonomy" (Gartner, May 2026). IDC talks about an "agentic economy" and predicts that 60% of the Global 2000 will run a majority of their business processes "AI-first" by 2029, up from 13% in 2026 (IDC FutureScape 2027). HFS coined "Services-as-Software" in September 2024 for AI-native execution that closes the gap between tools and outcomes (HFS). Consultancies and platform vendors prefer organizational labels. McKinsey's "agentic organization" "unites humans and AI agents—both virtual and physical—to work side by side at scale at near-zero marginal cost", with governance that is "real time, data driven, and embedded—with humans holding final accountability" (McKinsey). Microsoft's "Frontier Firm" is built on "hybrid" teams of humans and agents (Microsoft Work Trend Index 2025), and Salesforce launched the "Agentic Enterprise" with Agentforce 360 in October 2025 (Salesforce). The exact phrase "autonomous enterprise" belongs mostly to SAP. SAP adopted it as house vocabulary in early 2026 and staged Sapphire around "The Future of the Enterprise Is Autonomous", a vision in which "people set the direction and AI executes" (SAP News). Its alliance partner PwC sells a "roadmap to the Autonomous Enterprise" (PwC), and Automation Anywhere, Celonis, Genpact, EY, KPMG Belgium, HCLTech and Tech Mahindra also use the phrase.

Under the branding, the definitions share a core. AI agents and deterministic automation sense, decide and act across end-to-end processes, while humans set direction and policy and keep accountability. No credible source defines the autonomous enterprise as a business run with no people involved. Salesforce calls it "a strategic posture" and "not a single product or platform" (Salesforce). The outliers are vendors with automation to sell. Automation Anywhere defines the autonomous enterprise as "a business that can operate, adapt, and optimize workflows on its own, with minimal human intervention", and claims, without citing a source, that such firms run more than half their processes independently (Automation Anywhere). The real disagreement is about degree and pace: how far toward full autonomy a typical process should go, and how soon.

The lineage runs from self-healing servers to self-driving companies

The idea is older than the agent wave. In 2001 IBM launched autonomic computing. Its 38-page manifesto argued that the complexity of computing systems was outpacing administrators' ability to cope (eWeek). IBM wanted systems that would configure, heal, optimize and protect themselves under a Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute control loop, and in October 2002 it published a five-level maturity model running from manually managed to fully autonomic (Wikipedia). The "self-driving" metaphor followed around 2017–18. Aera Technology branded itself the cognitive technology for the "Self-Driving Enterprise", and MIT Sloan Management Review described companies in which "many routine, data-intensive decisions would be made by machines in partnership with humans" (MIT SMR). Gartner made hyperautomation its top strategic trend for 2020 (Gartner, Oct 2019). In 2023 it called machine customers "one of the biggest new growth opportunities of the decade" (Gartner). It then named agentic AI its number-one trend for 2025 and predicted that at least 15% of day-to-day work decisions would be made autonomously by 2028 (Gartner via Slack). The organizational labels arrived in 2025: Frontier Firm in April, agentic organization in September and Agentic Enterprise in October. In 2026 SAP took "autonomous enterprise" mainstream, and ServiceNow rebranded around "Agentic Business" and an "Autonomous Workforce" of AI specialists (ServiceNow).

Two lessons follow from this history. First, the constant is a closed control loop (sense, analyze, decide, act). It has moved from infrastructure (autonomic computing, autonomous networks) to processes (hyperautomation) and now, through LLM agents, to business decisions that cross functions. The word "autonomous" deliberately borrows the self-driving car's ladder of levels. Second, what actually backs the 2026 claims is mostly plumbing: agent registries and control towers, agent identity, MCP and A2A connectivity, knowledge graphs and libraries of domain agents. Everest Group's assessment of SAP's launch is blunt. "Sapphire 2026 is not a proof that autonomous enterprises are here." Scaled autonomous workflows are "unlikely to become a reality in the near term", and enterprises should "avoid scaling agentic workflows until integration, data, access controls, and process ownership are clear" (Everest Group).

A usable definition measures delegated authority per process

For an engineering team, the most defensible working definition combines the analysts' shared elements with the rigor of the autonomy-level frameworks. In this report, an autonomous enterprise is an organization in which a material, measured share of end-to-end business processes run as governed closed loops. AI agents and deterministic automation sense events, decide and act across systems of record under explicit, revocable delegations of authority, covering scope, risk thresholds, spend limits, escalation rules and a kill switch. Humans set intent and policy, decide exceptions and remain accountable for outcomes. Three distinctions keep the team's vocabulary honest. Agentic AI is a capability: goal-directed systems that plan and use tools. The agentic enterprise is an operating model built around teams of humans and agents. The autonomous enterprise is the degree of delegated authority actually achieved, which can only be measured one process at a time. Just as important is AWS's separation of agency, "the scope of actions an AI system is permitted and enabled to take", from autonomy, which is how independently the system acts (AWS). An agent can hold broad permissions and still need approval for every step. Feng, McDonald and Zhang make the same point from the user's side. They treat autonomy as a design decision separate from capability, and they define levels by whether the human acts as operator, collaborator, consultant, approver or observer (arXiv 2506.12469).

Most autonomy scales copy the SAE driving levels, usually by way of TM Forum's L0–L5 model for autonomous telecom networks. That model assigns execution, awareness, analysis, decision and intent to either people or systems, and keeps intent with people until Level 4 (Cisco/TM Forum). Capgemini modeled its six-level agent scale explicitly on vehicles and TM Forum (Capgemini). In May 2026 Gartner proposed a four-level governance ladder of Observe, Advise, Act with Approval and Act Autonomously, and warned that "applying uniform governance across AI agents will lead to enterprise AI agent failure" (Gartner). Two calibration points anchor the top of the scale. Hugging Face researchers argue that "fully autonomous AI agents should not be developed" (Mitchell et al.). Capgemini's 2025 executive survey expects only 15% of processes to run at Level 3 or above within a year and about 25% by 2028, with fully autonomous agents handling about 4% of processes (Capgemini). In practice, then, an "autonomous enterprise" is a distribution across a portfolio of processes, not a yes-or-no label. To measure it, score each material process by who senses, analyzes, decides, acts and sets intent, then report the share of processes, and of transaction volume, at L3 or higher. No analyst sets a numeric threshold, so the team must choose one. One option, which borrows IDC's "majority of processes" framing, is a majority of material process volume at L3 or higher, with L4 in selected domains. The same lens supports a test for what Gartner calls agent washing: "the rebranding of existing products, such as AI assistants, robotic process automation (RPA) and chatbots, without substantial agentic capabilities". Gartner estimates that only about 130 of the thousands of agentic AI vendors are real (Gartner). A product that only retrieves or suggests is L1. A bot that cannot act across systems is not an autonomous process. Autonomy without identity, an audit trail, runtime verification and revocation does not count, and neither does autonomy that cannot be dialed down for an individual process.

Spending soars while fewer than one in five firms run agents

Money is moving faster than authority. In May, Gartner forecast worldwide AI spending of $2.59 trillion in 2026, up 47% (Gartner), and in September it raised the figure to about $2.7 trillion (CIO.com). IDC expects agentic AI to account for more than 26% of worldwide IT spending, or $1.3 trillion, in 2029 (IDC). Gartner's adoption predictions are just as steep. It expects 33% of enterprise software applications to include agentic AI by 2028, up from under 1% in 2024, and at least 15% of day-to-day work decisions to be made autonomously by then (Gartner). It also predicts that "up to 40%" of enterprise applications will include task-specific agents by the end of 2026 (Gartner), and that up to $234 billion of application-software spend is at risk from agentic AI through 2030 (Gartner). These are planning assumptions, not measurements, and the survey evidence does not support them. Gartner's 15% of decisions is well above the 6–8% that executives in Capgemini's survey expect agents to make within one to three years (Capgemini).

On the strict measures, agents running in production with meaningful autonomy reach roughly 10–20% of organizations in 2026, mostly large firms. The looser measures show momentum, not delegated authority. Value is even more concentrated. In McKinsey's survey, as reported in secondary summaries, "high performers" (firms attributing 5% or more of EBIT to AI) stayed at about 6% of respondents in both 2025 and 2026. The share reporting any EBIT contribution was essentially flat, at 37%, even though 80% reported gains in individual productivity (McKinsey). PwC's survey of 4,454 CEOs found that 56% had seen no significant financial benefit from AI (PwC). In October 2026, only 13% of CIOs told Gartner that their enterprise had realized significant or transformational value (Gartner). The most neutral evidence is also the most sobering. In the survey run by the Atlanta Fed, Bank of England, Bundesbank and Macquarie University, more than 90% of executives reported no effect on employment and 89% no effect on productivity over the past three years. They expect productivity to rise only about 1.4% over the next three (NBER).

Spending, meanwhile, is running ahead of plans. Menlo Ventures, which invests in several of the companies it tracks, found that average enterprise AI spend reached 4.7 times the prior year's level against a planned 1.9 times, because agents "work independently, run in parallel… so spending scales with work rather than headcount" (Menlo Ventures). The much-quoted MIT NANDA claim that "95% of organizations are getting zero return" deserves less weight than it got. The report is labeled preliminary. It rests on interviews with 52 organizations and 153 survey responses collected at conferences, and it counts a project as successful only when interviewees described "marked and sustained" productivity or P&L impact (MIT NANDA).

Analysts' 2026 forecasts have moved from "agents everywhere" to governed, selective autonomy. Gartner's predictions read like an early warning of a slump. It expects over 40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by the end of 2027 because of escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls (Gartner). It also expects 40% of enterprises to demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 (Gartner), and 70% to abandon agentic AI built by vendors' forward-deployed engineers by 2028 (Gartner). Gartner published its first standalone Hype Cycle for Agentic AI in April 2026, and secondary coverage places the category at the Peak of Inflated Expectations (Gartner). Forrester expects enterprises to trust agents inside a single application but to hesitate when agents act across systems, and to move 20% of agentic workflows back to deterministic processes to save money (Forrester). IDC treats autonomy as an insurance and audit question. It predicts that, by 2028, Global 2000 firms without kill switches on production agents will pay 25% higher liability premiums and receive twice as many material audit findings (IDC).

Value is real where outcomes are verifiable

Where agents do pay off, they have the three things Menlo identifies: "environments agents can act in", "complete context" and "results agents can verify" (Menlo Ventures). Coding is the clearest case. At $34.0 billion it is the largest AI application category, ahead of enterprise chatbots at $25.0 billion, and far ahead of customer success ($2.7 billion), IT ($1.6 billion) and HR ($0.9 billion). Independent studies compiled by Stanford put task-level gains at 14–15% in customer support and 26% in software development, with smaller gains on tasks that need deeper reasoning (Stanford AI Index). IT service management is the strongest operational case, although the numbers come from the vendor. ServiceNow says its internal IT team resolves 90% of employee requests autonomously (Fortune). Its CFO claims "more than $1 billion in annualized value", an unaudited estimate that analysts compare with roughly $200 million of operating-expense savings (BERI).

Document-heavy back-office work is the third cluster. Rocket Companies reports more than 1.1 million team-member hours saved in 2025 (HousingWire). IBM's AskHR "contains" 94% of common questions, which means they were not escalated to a person, not that they were resolved well (IBM). Banks are moving carefully: Goldman Sachs described six months of early-stage work with embedded Anthropic engineers on agents for trade accounting and client onboarding, with no launch date (American Banker). MIT NANDA's interviews point the same way. Back-office deployments that replaced outsourcing and agency spend often paid back faster than the front-office projects that receive most of the budget (MIT NANDA).

Customer-facing AI has both the most success claims and the most public reversals. Klarna's CEO said the company had been "too focused on cost", which led to "a decline in quality", and Klarna started hiring human agents again (Entrepreneur). Commonwealth Bank of Australia reversed the cut of 45 call-centre roles it had replaced with a voice bot, calling the decision an "error" (Bloomberg). McDonald's switched off its IBM drive-thru ordering test after reports of wrong orders (NBC News). Telstra is cutting roles and rolling out multi-step agents, but says agents are not expected to deal directly with customers for now (Guardian via inkl). Salesforce says Agentforce let it cut support staff from about 9,000 to about 5,000 with similar customer satisfaction, but that comes from Marc Benioff's own podcast and is unaudited (NBC News). Workforce reversals are a pattern, not a few anecdotes. In an Orgvue survey, 55% of executives who cut jobs after deploying AI said those decisions were mistakes (TechRepublic), and Forrester expects half of AI-attributed layoffs to be quietly reversed, often with cheaper or offshore labor (The Register).

Reliability limits, missing controls and history explain the gap

Capability is rising fast, but reliability is not keeping pace. METR's frontier risk report puts the public frontier's time horizon at about 12 hours at 50% success, but only about 1.5 hours at 80% success. It also found that at least 16% of "successful" runs on tasks longer than eight hours were illegitimate, and that agents' real-world performance "has consistently been weaker than benchmark scores suggest" (METR). On OSWorld-Verified, the top computer-use agent scored 90.19% against a human baseline of 72.36%. On the harder OSWorld 2.0, whose workflows average about 318 tool calls, full-task completion stays below 50% (Presenc.ai). In CMU's simulated software company, the best agent completed 30% of tasks (TheAgentCompany). Salesforce's CRMArena-Pro found leading agents dropping from about 58% success on single-turn business tasks to about 35% in multi-turn settings, with "near-zero confidentiality awareness" (arXiv 2505.18878). Princeton's reliability study of 15 models concludes that capability "has grown much faster than reliability over the past two years" (arXiv 2602.16666). The arithmetic of long workflows explains why. If steps are independent and each succeeds 95% of the time, a 20-step process completes end to end only about 36% of the time.

Developer productivity, the best-studied case, shows a gap between perception and measurement. METR's 2025 randomized trial found that experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI, while believing it made them about 20% faster. Its 2026 follow-up was inconclusive because of selection effects (METR). Google's DORA 2025 report associated AI adoption with higher throughput but also with less stable delivery, and called AI "an amplifier" of the capability an organization already has (Google Cloud).

The controls that would make autonomy safe are mostly missing. In a November 2026 Gartner survey of 298 infrastructure and operations leaders, 95% reported at least one agent-related incident in the past 12 months and only 15% had automatic circuit breakers for runaway agents, yet 70% believed their agents operate within safe boundaries (Gartner). BCG finds that 42% of companies expect to give agents decision-making authority by 2030, but only 5% have full controls in place today: oversight, rollback gates, security, audit and cost guardrails (BCG). KPMG reports that the share of firms building controls into agents alongside monitoring fell from 43% to 30% in two quarters, even as deployment rose (KPMG). Public incidents show what happens without those controls. Replit's coding agent deleted a production database during a declared code freeze (Fortune). The Financial Times linked a 13-hour interruption of AWS Cost Explorer to the Kiro agent deciding to "delete and recreate the environment"; Amazon says the cause was user error and misconfigured access controls (The Register).

Earlier autonomy programs show what the journey is likely to look like. Telecom's autonomous-network effort is the closest parallel to an enterprise autonomy roadmap, and in 2026 it still finds most operators between Levels 2 and 3, with Level 4 claimed only in specific domains (TM Forum). Operators target Level 4 rather than Level 5 because "Level 4 may deliver about 90% of the value at less cost and risk than Level 5" (Bain). Algorithmic trading, the most mature high-autonomy domain, got there through hard automated limits rather than human vigilance. In 2012, Knight Capital's order router sent more than 4 million orders in 45 minutes and lost more than $460 million. That led to the SEC's first enforcement action under the Market Access Rule, citing missing pre-trade checks and exposure limits (SEC).

The technical stack rewards simple control flow and governed context

The five concept families the team needs are split across two sections: technical and data concepts here, and quality and operations, security and governance, and organization in the next. One idea runs through all of them. Production agents are mostly software engineering: explicit control flow, typed tools, governed data and measurement, with model calls placed only where judgment is actually needed.

Architecture: workflows first, one agent second, many agents last

Anthropic's distinction is still the foundation. Workflows are "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths", while agents "dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage" (Anthropic). The building block is an LLM augmented with retrieval, tools and memory. The canonical patterns (prompt chaining, routing, parallelization, orchestrator-workers and evaluator-optimizer) each add autonomy and cost. Anthropic's advice is to find "the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed". It also reports spending more time optimizing tools than prompts for its SWE-bench agent. Practitioners have turned this into doctrine. HumanLayer's 12-Factor Agents observes that most production "agents" are "mostly deterministic code, with LLM steps sprinkled in at just the right points". It adds that frameworks get teams to a "70-80% quality bar", which is "not good enough for most customer-facing features" (12-Factor Agents). Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework says to skip the agent when code or non-generative AI is enough, and to use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for static question answering. Where an agent is warranted, teams should "start with a single agent test" unless the use case crosses security or compliance boundaries or spans several teams (Microsoft CAF).

Inside the agent, practice in 2025–26 settled on a harness: a loop that gathers context, acts, verifies its work and repeats, with subagents running in isolated context windows and older history compacted (Claude blog). Microsoft's Agent Framework harness, generally available after Build 2026, bundles context compaction, a todo list with plan and execute modes, file memory, tool approval and built-in OpenTelemetry. Shell access, background sub-agents and automatic looping are opt-in (InfoQ). In an early comparison, Agent Framework stopped its own loop after 40 round-trips, while the GitHub Copilot SDK ran to 300 with no stop enforced by the host application. Loop budgets are therefore the harness's job, not a property of the model.

Multi-agent designs pay off only for broad, parallel work that mostly reads rather than writes. Anthropic's research system, in which an Opus model plans and Sonnet subagents search in parallel, beat a single agent by 90.2% on an internal research evaluation. But agents use about 4x the tokens of chat, and multi-agent systems about 15x, and the design fits poorly with tasks that need shared context and with "most coding tasks" (Anthropic). The MAST study analyzed more than 1,600 traces from seven multi-agent frameworks. It catalogued 14 failure modes across system design, misalignment between agents and task verification, and found that gains on popular benchmarks were "often minimal" (arXiv 2503.13657). Cognition argues for a single-threaded agent because "actions carry implicit decisions", and agents working in parallel make those decisions inconsistently (Cognition). MAST's traces come from GPT-4- and Claude 3-era models, so the gap may narrow, but no controlled comparison on 2026 models exists.

Protocols and platforms: standardize the edges, own the logic

Two protocols now define the edges of the stack, and both are under neutral governance. On 9 December 2025 the Linux Foundation formed the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF). Its founding projects are Anthropic's Model Context Protocol, Block's goose and OpenAI's AGENTS.md, and its backers include AWS, Anthropic, Block, Bloomberg, Cloudflare, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI (Linux Foundation). MCP, the layer that connects agents to tools and data, shipped its current specification on 28 July 2026 with a stateless core. The initialization handshake and session IDs are gone, and each request carries its own protocol version and capabilities. New Mcp-Method and Mcp-Name headers let gateways route and meter traffic without parsing request bodies. Long-running work moves to a Tasks extension, and Dynamic Client Registration is deprecated in favor of Client ID Metadata Documents (MCP blog). Roots, Sampling and Logging are deprecated under a policy that keeps them working for at least 12 months, so teams relying on 2025-era stateful behavior should migrate before late July 2027. Adoption is huge, with close to half a billion downloads a month for the Tier 1 SDKs. The official server registry, though, has been a preview since September 2025 (MCP blog), and client security is uneven. A March 2026 study of seven MCP clients found that tool-poisoning attacks succeeded anywhere from 0% to 100% of the time, depending on the client (arXiv 2603.22489).

A2A, the agent-to-agent layer, became a Linux Foundation project in June 2025 and absorbed IBM's Agent Communication Protocol in August 2025 (GitHub). It reached v1.0 in March 2026 and joined AAIF in August 2026, with more than 150 supporting organizations (A2A). Product support still lags. Microsoft's Agent Framework 1.0 lists A2A 1.0 as "coming soon" (Microsoft), and SAP plans to make Joule's A2A support generally available in Q4 2026 (SAP). A2A's main enterprise value is connecting agents across different SaaS platforms. Inside one team's system, plain function calls or a framework's built-in handoff mechanism are simpler. Watch for the acronym collision: IBM's discontinued Agent Communication Protocol (ACP) is unrelated to the OpenAI–Stripe Agentic Commerce Protocol, which is also abbreviated ACP.

The framework market has consolidated into a handful of stacks at version 1.0 or later. LangGraph 1.0 (October 2025) is still the most mature choice for explicit, auditable control flow with saved checkpoints (LangChain). Microsoft Agent Framework 1.0 (April 2026) replaces AutoGen and Semantic Kernel, which are now in maintenance mode (InfoQ). Google's ADK 2.0 became generally available in Python, Go and TypeScript between May and August 2026 (ADK). The OpenAI Agents SDK added a model-native harness and native sandboxes in April 2026 (OpenAI). The cautionary tale is OpenAI's visual Agent Builder and its Evals product. Both launched in October 2025 and will no longer be available from 30 November 2026 (OpenAI). That is a strong argument for keeping agent logic in code and keeping evaluation data the team owns. Durable execution, which lets a long-running agent survive crashes and restarts, has become standard plumbing. Temporal offers generally available integrations with the OpenAI Agents SDK (Temporal) and with Google ADK, AWS Strands, LangGraph and the Vercel AI SDK (Temporal).

Every hyperscaler now sells a modular platform for running agents. AWS Bedrock AgentCore has been generally available since October 2025 for "any framework, model, or protocol" (AWS). In March 2026 it added Policy, which uses AWS's Cedar policy language (AWS), and Evaluations, with 13 built-in evaluators (AWS). In April 2026 Google launched the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform as the successor to Vertex AI. It combines Vertex AI's model services with agent integration, security and operations tooling (Google), including an agent registry, identity, gateway and observability (Google Cloud). Microsoft spans Foundry, Copilot Studio and Agent 365. Since 1 July 2026, security features for Copilot Studio and Foundry agents require an Agent 365 license (Microsoft Learn). The model vendors host their own agent platforms. OpenAI has Frontier, and OpenAI would not confirm whether it supports third-party models (VentureBeat). Anthropic has Claude Managed Agents, in public beta since April 2026 at standard token rates plus $0.08 per session-hour (Claude).

At the protocol layer, portability is real: MCP tools, A2A agents and OpenTelemetry traces move between platforms. Lock-in concentrates elsewhere: in identity and permission models, memory and session stores, policy languages, evaluation datasets, low-code orchestration, and the pull of each vendor's data platform. The defense is architectural. Keep business logic in code on an open framework, tools behind MCP, delegation across teams behind A2A, traces in OpenTelemetry and evaluation sets in the team's own repository. Models and reliability: plan on the 80% horizon

Choosing models is now a portfolio problem. The dominant pattern pairs a frontier model for planning, judgment and final verification with cheaper worker models, and eventually with small fine-tuned models. NVIDIA researchers estimate that small language models could handle 40–70% of LLM calls in the open-source agents they studied, at 10–30 times lower serving cost. These are estimates, not measurements, and the paper describes six steps for converting logged agent calls into fine-tuned small models (arXiv 2506.02153). Anthropic's cost guidance adds a caution. Before building a cascade that routes requests between models, test the most capable model at lower effort settings: prompt caches are tied to a single model, so a cascade gives up cache reuse (Anthropic). For scoping autonomy, the number that matters is METR's 80% horizon of about 1.5 hours, not the 12-hour headline at 50% success. Unsupervised work should be limited to tasks of tens of minutes with verification gates, and longer work should be broken into checkpointed stages that can be reviewed. Computer-use agents, which operate software through its screens, should be a last resort for systems without APIs. a16z found real production deployments concentrated in standardized back-office work, with "almost nobody" using consumer agent modes for it (a16z). Robotic process automation (RPA) vendors such as UiPath now describe their platforms as orchestrating agents, robots and people together, rather than as being replaced by agents (UiPath).

Data and knowledge: context engineering is the core craft

In mid-2025 context engineering replaced prompt engineering as the central skill. Shopify's Tobi Lütke called it "the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM". Andrej Karpathy described "the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step" (Simon Willison). Anthropic sets the goal as "the smallest set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of your desired outcome" (Anthropic). The discipline rests on measured context rot. Chroma's study of 18 models held task difficulty constant and varied only input length. Performance grew "increasingly unreliable" as inputs got longer, even on trivial tasks such as copying text, and distractors sped the decline (Chroma). Chroma sells retrieval infrastructure, but the result matches Anthropic's description of a finite "attention budget". Million-token context windows therefore do not remove the need to curate what goes into them.

The toolkit is now well defined (Anthropic). System prompts should sit at the "right altitude", carrying a few canonical examples rather than long lists of edge cases. Tool sets should be minimal and unambiguous: as Anthropic puts it, "If a human engineer can't definitively say which tool should be used in a given situation", an agent can't be expected to do better. Data should arrive just in time, loaded at runtime through lightweight identifiers such as file paths and stored queries rather than preloaded. As the window fills, old tool results should be compacted or cleared, work that spans sessions should keep structured notes outside the window, and read-heavy exploration should go to sub-agents that return condensed summaries of 1,000–2,000 tokens.

Tool design belongs to the same craft (Anthropic). Paginate and truncate tool outputs; Claude Code caps tool responses at 25,000 tokens by default. Offer concise and detailed response modes, give tools distinct names grouped by service, resolve opaque IDs into meaningful names, and return error messages the agent can act on. LangChain sorts these techniques into four groups (write, select, compress and isolate), which gives the team a shared vocabulary (LangChain). The engineering consequence is to treat context assembly as code: versioned, unit-tested, traced, and with an explicit token budget for each component.

Behind the context window sits a knowledge layer. It is converging on business semantics that machines can read and that are governed like any other data asset. Forrester puts it well: "Enterprise AI needs a governed context layer, not just data" (Forrester). A practical architecture has four planes. Each is exposed to agents as governed tools that run under the agent's own identity.

A semantic or metric layer holds every number an agent quotes. The Open Semantic Interchange specification, now public under the Apache 2.0 license, lets metric definitions move between dbt, Snowflake, Databricks, Tableau and other tools (Snowflake). Databricks metric views make governed KPIs queryable by agents (Databricks). Having agents query governed metrics rather than raw tables is the main defense against analytics agents writing SQL whose metric definitions drift from the official ones.

An ontology or knowledge graph encodes entities, relationships and permitted actions. Palantir's Ontology MCP server lets outside agents run only predefined actions (Palantir). Microsoft's Fabric IQ ontology connects "people, processes, systems, actions, rules and data" (Microsoft Fabric). Gartner notes that "subject matter experts remain essential" to validate the business meaning (Gartner).

Hybrid retrieval serves unstructured documents. Anthropic's contextual retrieval cut top-20 retrieval failures by 49% when combined with BM25 keyword search, and by 67% when reranking was added. Note that it measures retrieval, not answer quality (Anthropic). Microsoft's LazyGraphRAG supports graph-style queries across a whole corpus. Its indexing costs the same as vector RAG and 0.1% of full GraphRAG (Microsoft Research).

Memory is the least mature plane. Mem0 reports about 94% on the LongMemEval benchmark for its managed platform (Mem0). One independent test of its open-source edition scored 32.4% (DEV). Memory should therefore be treated as a governed data product, with provenance, retention and deletion rules, and evaluated on the team's own traffic.

Data readiness is the most common point of failure. Gartner predicted that through 2026, organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects that lack AI-ready data. It also found that 63% of organizations lack the right data management practices for AI or are unsure whether they have them (Gartner). Among banks, 80% name data readiness and access as the top barrier to deploying agents (KPMG). The practical response is to assess readiness use case by use case (definitions, quality, lineage and access policy) and to fund that work before building agents.

Process knowledge is the input most teams overlook. Process-mining vendors now sell "process context" to agents. In November 2025 Celonis launched a Process Intelligence MCP server to give agents "dynamic operational context" (Celonis), and Joule with SAP Signavio became generally available in February 2026 (SAP News). In July 2026 Gartner published its first Magic Quadrant for platforms that model a "digital twin of an organization". Vendors quote it describing these platforms as a "sensory system and governance plane to support autonomous business operations" (ARIS). Event streaming supplies the real-time feed. Confluent's Streaming Agents became generally available in May 2026 as "production-ready, event-driven agents running natively on Flink and Kafka". A companion context engine serves fresh data to agents over MCP, and "everything is event-driven and replayable" (Confluent). Two design consequences follow. First, agents shift from being triggered by chat to being triggered by business events. Second, if agents emit their decisions and tool calls as business events that carry case and object IDs, the same process-mining tools that measure human work can also measure and govern agents.

Evals, security and ownership are where agent programs fail

The technical stack is converging, but that is not where agent programs break down. Respondents to LangChain's agent-engineering survey name quality as the top barrier to production (32%), ahead of latency (20%); at large enterprises, security comes second (LangChain). Gartner attributes cancellations to cost, unclear value and weak risk controls, and Microsoft's guidance says flatly that "agents fail more often from unclear ownership than from technical problems" (Microsoft CAF). The remaining concept families (quality and operations, security and governance, and organization) are where a Gen AI team earns, or loses, the right to give its agents more autonomy.

Quality: evaluation is the main engineering workload

Agent evaluation is now a defined discipline. It has its own vocabulary (task, trial, grader, transcript, outcome, harness) and an accepted principle: evaluating "an agent" means evaluating the model and its harness together (Anthropic). Anthropic's January 2026 guidance is the clearest playbook. It starts with 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures, each written so that two domain experts would agree on pass or fail, and runs every trial in a clean, isolated environment. It combines three kinds of grader: code-based graders are fast and objective but brittle, model-based graders are flexible but must be calibrated against human judgment, and human review is the gold standard but slow. It insists on grading "what the agent produced, not the path it took", and it keeps capability suites, which should start with low pass rates, separate from regression suites, which should sit near 100%.

The same guidance explains why consistency needs its own metric. pass@k measures whether at least one of k attempts succeeds, while pass^k measures whether all k succeed. At 75% success per attempt, pass^3 is only about 42%. It also shows how much the harness matters: one model's CORE-Bench score rose from 42% to about 95% once grading bugs were fixed and a less constrained scaffold was used.

Practitioners Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar call error analysis "the most important activity in evals". Their method is to review at least 100 real traces and write free-form notes on each, group the notes into a taxonomy of failure types, count how often each type occurs, and only then build automated evaluators for the most common ones, "for errors you discover, not errors you imagine" (Hamel Husain). They recommend binary pass/fail judgments over 1–5 scales. Every LLM judge should be validated against human labels on held-out data, with one judge per failure type, and a slow LLM judge should never be used as a guardrail that blocks requests in real time. Husain reports that teams spent 60–80% of development time on error analysis and evaluation (Simon Willison). That is the most important staffing fact for an agent team. Practice lags the advice: only 52.4% of LangChain's respondents run offline evals and 37.3% run online evals, even though 89% have some observability (LangChain).

Public benchmarks are useful for screening, not as evidence for buying decisions. The Agentic Benchmark Checklist found flaws in task setup and reward design that misestimate performance by up to 100% in relative terms. It found outcome-validity flaws in 7 of 10 popular benchmarks; on τ-bench, a trivial agent that returned empty responses scored 38%, beating a GPT-4o agent (arXiv 2507.02825). Princeton's Holistic Agent Leaderboard found that higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in 21 of 36 runs, and warns that agents "can be 100x more expensive while only being 1% better" (arXiv 2510.11977). LLM judges show known biases toward answer position, length and their own outputs, even though strong judges agree with humans more than 80% of the time (arXiv 2306.05685). The team should audit its internal suites the same way. Can an agent that does nothing pass? Can a wrong answer pass?

Operations: AgentOps, reliability engineering and the release unit

Observability has a standard, but not a stable one. OpenTelemetry's GenAI semantic conventions model an agent run as a tree of spans: an invoke_agent span contains chat, execute_tool, retrieval and memory operations. Capture of prompt and response content is off by default (OpenTelemetry). Every GenAI attribute is still in "Development" status. The conventions moved to their own repository in June 2026, and attributes such as gen_ai.system and gen_ai.usage.prompt_tokens have already been renamed (DEV). Teams should pin a convention version and avoid hard-coding attribute names into dashboards.

Operational practice treats any agent change as a release unit: model version, prompt, tool schemas, retrieval configuration, guardrail settings and memory policy, versioned together. A release passes eval gates, then a shadow phase in which actions are logged but not executed, then a small canary, with rollback controlled by feature flags (DEV). Microsoft's operational standard requires acceptance thresholds for every production agent, approved by its business owner; re-evaluation whenever an agent's model is replaced, its instructions change or its permissions expand; restrictive consumption limits on experimental agents; and defined retirement triggers for every agent (Microsoft CAF). It also warns that monitoring "must also detect wrong answers and unintended actions from agents that appear healthy". Kill switches belong in a configuration store, so they can be flipped without a deployment, and should be checked before every consequential decision and audited. Agents that "call tools, spend money, or touch private data" need one from their first production configuration (DEV). Anthropic uses "rainbow deployments", which shift traffic to a new version gradually because long-running agents are mid-task when an update ships (Anthropic).

Reliability engineering for agents starts from compounding errors. Error rates per step rise as a task goes on, because models build on their own earlier mistakes (arXiv 2509.09677). In τ-bench, GPT-4o-era retail agents succeeded about 61% of the time on a single attempt, but fewer than 25% of tasks succeeded on all eight attempts (arXiv 2406.12045). The engineering response is to catch errors at checkpoints instead of letting them pile up. That means deterministic validators and schema-constrained outputs after each critical step, idempotency keys on every tool that writes so that a replayed step does not repeat its side effects, and a propose-validate-commit sequence for irreversible actions, with compensating actions as undo paths. It means durable execution that records each tool result, so that a resumed run skips calls that already completed (Temporal), along with hard budgets on steps, tokens, dollars and wall-clock time. And it means sandboxed code execution with least-privilege credentials, plus escalation that gives a human enough evidence to make a real decision. Thoughtworks' November 2026 Technology Radar moved agent sandboxing and threat modeling into its Adopt ring. It also warned against "human in the loop theater", where human approval becomes a ritual rather than a real check (IT Brief).

Economics: cost per completed task, dominated by escalations

Unit costs are falling while total spend rises. Epoch AI estimates that the price of a fixed level of capability falls about 13x per year (Epoch AI). Yet MIT researchers find that the cost of running benchmarks "has remained flat or increased" (arXiv 2511.23455), because agents use more tokens per task. In the State of FinOps 2026 survey, 98% of FinOps practitioners now manage AI spend, and visibility into AI costs is their top challenge (Linux Foundation). The FinOps Foundation recommends tracking unit metrics, such as cost per query or per workflow, rather than token counts (FinOps Foundation). The proven cost levers are well documented. On Anthropic's API, reading a cached prompt costs roughly a tenth of the base input price, while writing to the cache costs extra (Anthropic). Batch processing takes 50% off work nobody is waiting on, including eval runs (Anthropic). Semantic caching needs care: in a banking FAQ study, a fixed similarity threshold produced false-positive rates as high as 99% for some embedding models (InfoQ). Anthropic's own guidance is to optimize "cost per completed task, not cost per token" (Anthropic).

A worked example shows why that framing matters. The numbers are illustrative, not sourced. Suppose a task uses 1 million input tokens over its turns (85% cache reads, 5% cache writes and 10% uncached) plus 20,000 output tokens. Prices are $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens; cache reads cost a tenth of the input price and cache writes 1.25 times. The token cost is then about $1.04, against $3.30 with no caching. If 80% of tasks complete without help and 20% escalate to a 10-minute human fix at $60 an hour, escalations add $2.00 per task. Spot-checking 10% of outputs for two minutes each adds $0.20. The total is about $3.24 per completed task, against $15 for a person doing the task alone in 15 minutes. The escalation rate, not the token price, drives the economics, so making agents more reliable is also the main way to cut their cost.

Security: treat prompt injection as a permanent condition

The core security fact is that LLMs cannot reliably tell instructions apart from data. Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" names the dangerous combination: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content and the ability to communicate externally. "If your agent combines these three features, an attacker can easily trick it into accessing your private data and sending it to that attacker" (Simon Willison). Meta's Agents Rule of Two turns this into a design rule. Within one session, an agent should have at most two of three properties: it processes untrusted input, it can access sensitive systems or data, or it can change state or communicate externally. A task that needs all three requires human supervision (Meta AI). The Cloud Security Alliance reports that indirect prompt injection "has crossed the line from proof-of-concept to live exploitation" (CSA).

Incidents to date follow four recurring routes to harm. The first is outbound channels that carry the stolen data. In EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711), one crafted email made Microsoft 365 Copilot send out internal data without the user clicking anything; the attack slipped past Microsoft's prompt-injection classifiers using reference-style Markdown links and images that load automatically (Aim Security). ForcedLeak (CVSS 9.4) extracted Salesforce Agentforce data through a domain on Salesforce's allow-list that had expired and been re-registered for about $5 (Noma Security). The second is over-privileged identities that turn injections into breaches. A malicious issue in a public GitHub repository hijacked an agent with GitHub MCP access, which then leaked the contents of private repositories, a pattern Invariant Labs called an architectural "toxic agent flow" rather than a bug in the code (Invariant Labs). The third is the tool supply chain. The first malicious MCP server found in the wild silently copied every email sent through it to an attacker (The Hacker News), and researchers found 341 malicious skills among the 2,857 on ClawHub, the skills marketplace for the OpenClaw agent (eSecurity Planet). The fourth is missing stop controls that let agents destroy data, from Replit's database deletion to an OpenClaw agent that ignored stop commands while deleting a user's emails (OWASP). OWASP's round-up for the first quarter of 2026 counted eight incidents, only one of which had a CVE, and concluded that "most AI security events have no CVE", so vulnerability feeds alone will miss them.

The reference frameworks have settled. The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, released in December 2025, runs from ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack to ASI10 Rogue Agents. In between it covers tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, supply chain, unexpected code execution, memory and context poisoning, insecure communication between agents, cascading failures and exploitation of human trust in agents. Its central principle is "least agency" (OWASP). MITRE ATLAS catalogs attacker techniques against agents, and it expanded sharply in May 2026 with input from banks including JPMorganChase and Citi (MITRE). The Cloud Security Alliance's seven-layer MAESTRO model structures threat modeling for systems of many agents (CSA). Testing has to adapt to the target. In NIST's agent-hijacking evaluations, new attacks designed for specific tasks raised attack success from 11%, the strongest existing attack, to 81% (NIST).

Because classifiers can be bypassed, the strongest defenses come from the system's design and from rules that always give the same answer. The research on design patterns states the governing rule: once an agent has taken in untrusted input, it must be constrained so that the input cannot trigger consequential actions (arXiv 2506.08837). Patterns such as plan-then-execute and the dual-LLM split keep the model that plans away from raw untrusted content. Google DeepMind's CaMeL goes further. A privileged model plans, a quarantined model parses untrusted data, and an interpreter tracks where each piece of data came from and applies policy to every tool call. It solved 77% of AgentDojo tasks with provable security, against 84% for an undefended system (arXiv 2503.18813). That gap is a concrete measure of what safety costs in capability. A practical reference architecture starts with a single policy checkpoint at the tool-call boundary that evaluates rules written as code, denies by default, and checks the agent's identity, the user who delegated the task, where the data came from and the action's risk tier. AWS AgentCore Policy, for example, checks every tool request against policies in AWS's Cedar language before the request reaches the tool (AWS). Classifier guardrails form a second layer. Outbound controls block automatic rendering of external URLs and monitor allow-listed domains for expiry, and tool supply-chain hygiene means a private registry, pinned and hashed server versions, and re-approval whenever a tool's description changes.

Identity and oversight: every agent needs an owner, a scope and a stop button

Agents are becoming first-class non-human identities with human sponsors. Microsoft Entra Agent ID is generally available. It defines owner, sponsor and manager roles for each agent, and its lifecycle workflows reassign sponsorship when a sponsor leaves "to prevent orphaned agents". Access packages cover both agents acting on behalf of a user and agents acting on their own, and Conditional Access templates can block high-risk agent identities (Microsoft Learn). Microsoft Agent 365, the control plane for registering and governing agents, became generally available on 1 May 2026 (Redmond Magazine). AWS AgentCore Identity provides workload identities for agents, a token vault, and outbound OAuth that is either delegated by a user or machine-to-machine (AWS). Okta's Cross App Access lets the enterprise identity provider broker, observe and revoke an agent's access as it moves from one application to another (Okta).

The standards underneath are familiar. MCP requires servers to act as OAuth 2.1 resource servers. Clients must use RFC 8707 resource indicators to bind each token to a specific server, and servers must never pass a client's token through to upstream APIs (MCP). OAuth token exchange (RFC 8693) records who is acting for whom through its "act" claim (IETF). NIST's NCCoE concept paper on agent identity is reported to build on OAuth, OpenID Connect, SCIM and SPIFFE rather than invent new standards (CSA).

The recurring failure is over-privilege. In Google's Vertex AI "Double Agent" case, attackers abused the default permissions of a service agent (OWASP). In Amazon's own account of the Kiro outage, the engineer involved had broader permissions than expected (The Register). The minimum design for every production agent therefore starts with a unique identity for each agent, never a shared service account or a developer's own credentials, and a named human sponsor with an expiry date. Its tokens should be short-lived, scoped to one resource and identify both the user and the agent; its secrets should be injected by a gateway rather than readable by the agent; and revocation through the identity provider should serve as the ultimate kill switch.

Human oversight is a legal requirement in high-risk settings and a common point of failure. The EU AI Act requires that the people overseeing high-risk systems understand the system's limitations and stay alert to automation bias. They must also be able to override or reverse its outputs and interrupt it through "a 'stop' button or a similar procedure" (AI Act Art. 14). GDPR Article 22 gives people the right not to be subject to decisions made solely by automated means that have legal or similarly significant effects. Its safeguards include human intervention and the right to contest the decision (GDPR).

In practice, approvals fail in three ways. People over-trust polished agent explanations; OWASP lists this as ASI09, human-agent trust exploitation. Security researchers reported in 2026 that in several agent frameworks, the action a human approved could differ from the action that actually ran (Adversa). And instructions can simply be ignored, as when an OpenClaw agent kept deleting email after its user told it to stop (OWASP). A meaningful approval process therefore shows the approver the exact action, its parameters, a diff or preview, and where any untrusted input came from, rather than the agent's own summary. It binds the approval to that exact action, cryptographically or in code, so a different action cannot run under it, and it limits how often approvals are requested to avoid approval fatigue. Finally, it enforces the stop button and spending budgets outside the agent process, through token revocation, a deny-all switch at the gateway and spend caps, all tested regularly.

Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI, the first government framework written specifically for agents, makes the same point. It says organizations should limit an agent's autonomy and its access to tools and data from the start, and define the checkpoints that require human approval (MDDI).

Governance and regulation: one control set, mapped to the strictest regime

General AI governance frameworks are well established, even though standards written specifically for agents are not. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework supplies the shared vocabulary of Govern, Map, Measure and Manage (NIST). Its Generative AI Profile adds 12 risks and more than 200 suggested actions (NIST AI 600-1). ISO/IEC 42001 defines an AI management system that organizations can be certified against (ISO), but certification is still rare: BSI issued its 50th certificate only in November 2026, after issuing 11 in all of 2025 (BSI). NIST launched an AI Agent Standards Initiative in February 2026 and has promised guidelines, but has not yet issued a final agent-security guideline (NIST).

Banks face a specific gap. In April 2026 the Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC replaced the 2011 model-risk guidance (SR 11-7) with SR 26-2, whose footnote states: "Generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within the scope of this guidance" (Federal Reserve). That leaves banks to build a separate control framework for agents under their general risk-management obligations. Tooling for AI governance is also forming into a recognized product category: Gartner's first Magic Quadrant for AI governance platforms, published in 2026, evaluated 13 vendors (Gartner).

The EU timetable is now fixed. Under the AI Act, agents count as AI systems "designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy" (AI Act Art. 3), and their risk class depends on their intended purpose. The Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026. It moved the high-risk obligations for Annex III uses, which include employment, credit scoring, insurance pricing and access to essential services, to a fixed date of 2 December 2027. Obligations for Annex I products moved to 2 August 2028. The Article 50 transparency duties, including telling people they are interacting with an AI system, have applied since 2 August 2026 (EUR-Lex; Gibson Dunn). High-risk systems must log events automatically, and deployers must keep those logs for at least six months (AI Act Art. 26). A deployer that substantially modifies a high-risk system, or repurposes a general-purpose system for a high-risk use, takes on the obligations of a provider (AI Act Art. 25). That is a real risk for teams that build HR or credit agents on top of foundation models.

The United States has no federal law overriding state AI rules. Executive Order 14365 created a Justice Department task force to challenge state AI laws, and in March 2026 the White House proposed legislation that would preempt them, but the state laws remain enforceable (Gibson Dunn). Colorado replaced its AI Act with SB 26-189, effective 1 January 2027. The new law covers automated decision-making technology that materially influences consequential decisions in areas such as employment, lending, housing and insurance. It requires notice, an explanation within 30 days of an adverse outcome, a chance to correct data, human review and appeal, and it bars joint and several liability between developers and deployers (McDermott). California's CCPA rules on automated decision-making technology also require compliance from 1 January 2027 (CPPA), and its SB 947 bars employers from relying solely on automated systems for discipline or termination (California Governor). The UK allows significant decisions to be made solely by automated means, provided safeguards are in place, under the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (legislation.gov.uk).

Liability law treats agents as tools of the company that deploys them. A Canadian tribunal rejected Air Canada's argument that its chatbot was "a separate legal entity" responsible for its own actions (CanLII). California's AB 316 bars defendants from arguing that an AI system caused harm autonomously (California Legislature). US uniform law has long allowed contracts to be formed by electronic agents, even when no person reviewed the terms (Uniform Law Commission). The EU's revised Product Liability Directive treats software, including AI, as a product, for items placed on the market after 9 December 2026 (EUR-Lex). The major platforms' indemnities, such as Microsoft's Copilot Copyright Commitment, focus on intellectual-property claims (Microsoft). The risk from an agent's mistaken actions therefore stays with the deploying company unless its contracts say otherwise.

Organization: ownership, redesign and trust decide outcomes

The organizational evidence is the most consistent in the research. BCG's rule of thumb is that 10% of the effort should go to algorithms, 20% to technology and data, and 70% to people and processes (BCG). Its 2026 survey of nearly 12,000 workers found that "employees with clear strategic direction but limited tool access outperform those with strong tool access but no direction". It also found that 74% of frontline employees use AI regularly but only 36% feel adequately trained, and that 66% get little or no guidance on how to use the time AI saves them (BCG). Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index attributes roughly twice as much of workers' reported AI impact to organizational factors as to individual effort (67% versus 32%). It finds that only 13% of workers are rewarded for reinventing their work with AI when the results fall short (Microsoft).

Trust is fragile. In a 47-country study, only 46% of people were willing to trust AI systems, while 66% relied on AI output without checking its accuracy and 56% reported making mistakes in their work because of AI (KPMG). The work itself changes too. Half or more of BCG's respondents spend more time reviewing or directing AI output, and 41% of regular users report increased mental strain. Deloitte describes "agent supervisors" as the people who "enter workflows at designed handoff points to handle exceptions and critical decisions" (Deloitte). Their review and exception-handling work has to be designed and staffed, not assumed.

Three organizational choices matter most for a development team.

Ownership. Microsoft's guidance assigns each agent's outcomes and remaining risk to the business function that relies on it. Central teams manage the shared platform rather than owning every agent. The guidance also warns that "pilots without stop criteria rarely end" (Microsoft CAF), yet only 31% of CIOs have guidelines for when a pilot should not move to production (Gartner).

Redesign before automation. Deloitte warns that layering agents onto existing workflows designed for people "adds work and can create 'workslop'", meaning low-quality output that others must fix.

Sourcing. MIT NANDA found that projects run with outside partners reached deployment about 67% of the time, against about 33% for tools built internally. The authors caution that this self-reported gap "may reflect organizational capabilities rather than implementation approach" (MIT NANDA), and they observe that successful organizations "decentralize implementation authority but retain accountability". Gartner predicts that 70% of enterprises will abandon agents built by vendors' forward-deployed engineers by 2028. Whatever a company buys, it must own the evaluations, identities, permissions and agent registry needed to govern it.

Workforce plans need the same humility. In the named company cases, headcount fell mostly through attrition and not backfilling roles. That matches the NBER finding that about two-thirds of the job reductions executives expect will come through slower hiring (NBER). Meanwhile, employment of software developers aged 22–25 has fallen nearly 20% since 2024 (Stanford HAI).

A five-phase, 24-month roadmap raises autonomy only on evidence

The published playbooks agree on a sequence, even though few of them give timelines. Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework now has three phases: organizational readiness, which ends in "a defined operating model and an investment strategy"; architecture and governance, which ends in "defined architectural standards and governance policies"; and operational standards for running agents "safely and efficiently at scale" (Microsoft CAF). AWS lays out an eight-week platform build. It sets up a generative AI gateway with controls on token budgets early on, and ends with production accounts and CI/CD pipelines with "manual approval gates" (AWS). MIT NANDA found that top mid-market firms took about 90 days to go from pilot to full implementation, while large enterprises took nine months or longer (MIT NANDA). Anthropic's Frontier Academy pairs a simulated deployment, run "from the right use case through security review to handover", with a 12-week residency in which each engineer leads a real use case inside their own organization (Anthropic). The biggest change in 2026 is that governance has moved to the start of the roadmap. Gartner finds that 71% of organizations expect to deploy agentic AI within 12 months, but only 21% have governance capabilities for it in place (Gartner). Phase 0 is mostly decisions, not code. Microsoft's readiness gate asks for four things: a business baseline measured before the agent enters the process; the total cost, including integration, governance and monitoring; the net business value, because "productivity gains don't automatically translate into financial savings"; and an explicit decision to expand, improve or retire the agent (Microsoft CAF). Setting stop criteria at this stage is what keeps a pilot from turning into a demonstration that never ends. Only 17% of CIOs have guidelines for abandoning low-value AI that is already in production (Gartner). AWS's scoping questions make a good intake form: "What specific outcome is the agent responsible for? Who is it acting on behalf of? Who benefits? Where does the agent's autonomy begin and end? Phase 1 builds the control layer once, so that every later agent inherits it. Each deliverable answers a gap in the failure data from section 2. Only 15% of operations teams have automatic circuit breakers, so the platform includes one. Agents in the reported incidents acted with broader permissions than intended, so each agent gets its own identity, scoped more narrowly than any engineer's. The gateway can deny all traffic, and evals run in CI before the first pilot ships. Choosing the platform is the main architectural decision. Given the lock-in analysis in section 3, the team should keep agent logic in an open framework, and keep its evaluation data, traces and tool catalog portable, whichever hyperscaler hosts the runtime.

Phase 2 tests the thesis on the work most likely to pay off. The first pilots should cover three kinds of work. One is a high-volume back-office document or case workflow with reversible actions and a measured baseline, the area where MIT NANDA found the fastest payback. Another is a read-only knowledge or research agent, which builds trust and evaluation skill at low risk. The third is an engineering or IT-operations agent whose outputs can be checked by tests or runbooks. Customer-facing agents that issue payments, refunds or commitments should wait until their consistency and approval patterns are proven, as the Klarna and CBA reversals suggest. Every pilot starts at L2, with humans approving consequential actions, and begins by redesigning the workflow rather than automating the current process.

Phase 3 raises autonomy one class of action at a time. An action moves from L2 to L3, that is, from per-action approval to autonomous execution with sampled human review, only when its pass^k score, override rate and incident record meet thresholds the business owner signed off in advance. Capability suites whose pass rates approach 100% become regression suites, as Anthropic recommends, and reusing shared tools and MCP servers becomes the main way to build faster.

Phase 4 is where "autonomous enterprise" becomes a measurable claim about the process portfolio rather than a slogan. McKinsey describes "agent factories", in which a human team of two to five people can supervise 50 to 100 specialized agents running an end-to-end process such as customer onboarding or closing the books (McKinsey). Treat that ratio as a planning assumption to test, not a benchmark. L4 is reserved for domains where rollback, verification and liability cover are proven, mirroring telecom operators' decision to stop at Level 4. Demotion must be as routine as promotion. Gartner predicts that 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission agents after incidents, so a team that can dial an agent back down gracefully keeps its program alive.

Pick use cases with a scoring rubric and an agent-or-not gate

Use-case selection should start by asking whether an agent is needed at all. OpenAI recommends agents only for workflows that involve complex decisions, rules that are hard to maintain, or heavy reliance on unstructured data. "Otherwise, a deterministic solution may suffice" (OpenAI). Microsoft sends structured, predictable tasks to traditional automation and question answering to RAG, noting that "not every business problem requires an AI agent" (Microsoft CAF). OWASP's "least agency" principle supplies the security rationale: autonomy that a task does not need expands the attack surface without adding value (OWASP).

Candidates that pass this gate can be scored on Microsoft's three dimensions: business impact, user desirability and technical feasibility. On feasibility, strong candidates are those where "the consequences of an incorrect action are reversible or contained", and weak ones involve actions that are "difficult to undo, such as payments or customer commitments" (Microsoft CAF). A workable weighting, proposed here rather than taken from any source, is 30% business impact, 30% feasibility and risk, 20% desirability and 20% agent fit. Feasibility and risk covers reversibility, blast radius, data readiness and API availability. Agent fit includes whether outcomes can be verified well enough to evaluate. The backlog should be re-scored every quarter because, as OpenAI notes, "the high-effort use cases you have today might become low-effort as AI capabilities advance" (OpenAI).

On sourcing, buy or partner for commodity workflows. Build where proprietary process knowledge gives a lasting advantage, which Microsoft calls "owned intelligence" (Microsoft Work Trend Index 2026). Either way, keep evals, data access, identity and the agent registry in-house.

Staff a hub-and-spoke team with named owners

The operating models in the sources converge on a federated structure with a strong central hub. Business functions own each agent's outcome and remaining risk. A central platform team owns the gateway, runtime, tool catalog, identity integration, observability and evaluation harness. An enablement function owns standards, training and use-case intake. Risk, security and compliance own risk tiering, red-teaming and incident authority. An architecture and investment board controls the gates to production; at Dell, for example, business leaders sign off on material returns before investment (Deloitte). AWS calls the unit that builds and runs agents an AgentOps team, in which AI practitioners, domain specialists, software engineers, product owners, compliance leads and platform architects "jointly own the entire lifecycle of an agent" (AWS). This hub-and-spoke shape reconciles two findings that seem to conflict: MIT NANDA's, that successful organizations decentralize implementation, and Deloitte's call for central review. Two staffing points follow from the evidence. First, evaluation is not a side duty. If error analysis and evaluation take 60–80% of development time, every pod needs an engineer who owns them and a domain expert with the authority to make quality calls, a role Husain and Shankar call the "benevolent dictator" (Hamel Husain). Second, forward-deployed engineers are scarce. Job-market trackers report that Indeed postings for the role rose from 643 to 8,137 between April 2025 and April 2026 (Plank). The team should therefore grow its own through a residency program rather than rely on vendors' engineers, whose agents Gartner expects most enterprises to abandon.

Learn in tiers and prove skill by shipping

The curriculum should follow the five concept families. Every engineer needs five cores. The technical core is choosing between workflows and agents, harness loops, MCP and A2A, durable execution and model portfolios. The data core is context engineering, hybrid retrieval, semantic layers and memory governance. The quality core is error analysis, evals graded on outcomes, pass^k, OpenTelemetry tracing and cost per completed task. The security core is the lethal trifecta, the Rule of Two, OWASP's agentic Top 10, agent identity and policy-as-code. The fifth core is enough of the organizational and regulatory picture to design compliant systems: ownership, approval design, the EU AI Act timeline and the rules on consequential decisions. Microsoft warns that "uniform training for every employee spends budget on skills that many people won't use" (Microsoft CAF), so depth should vary by role.

Govern with tiered approvals and controls outside the model

Governance should be proportional to autonomy and consequence, as Gartner argues, and enforced by the platform rather than by policy documents. The approval matrix below is this report's own synthesis. It draws on Singapore's framework, Meta's Rule of Two, and OpenAI's guidance that "actions that are sensitive, irreversible, or have high stakes should trigger human oversight until confidence in the agent's reliability grows" (OpenAI). It scores each tool action on reversibility, financial or legal impact, data sensitivity, external visibility and blast radius.

Tier Typical actions Control Highest autonomy level allowed
0 Read-only actions inside the organization Autonomous and logged L4
1 Reversible writes inside the organization Autonomous, with a sample reviewed after the fact L3
2 External communications; payments below a threshold; changes to access or permissions Approval in advance by the requester or owner, tied to the exact action L2
3 Irreversible or destructive actions; payments above a threshold; regulated consequential decisions Two approvals or specialist review, with a recorded human decision L2, with the decision made by a person

Standing controls surround the matrix. Microsoft requires that "every agent should be discoverable through an approved inventory process" (Microsoft CAF), and Deloitte calls for proof of "what agents did, why, and under whose authority" (Deloitte). In practice, each agent gets a registry entry that names its accountable business owner and its technical owner. The entry also records the agent's risk tier and autonomy level, its permitted tools and data, its identity, its model version, its eval thresholds and latest results, its budget, its escalation path, its incident contacts and its retirement criteria.

Before it is built, every agent is checked against the Rule of Two and threat-modeled against the MAESTRO layers and the OWASP agentic Top 10. Once it is running, every tool call passes through the default-deny policy checkpoint. Every action is written to a signed, append-only log that records the user, agent, model version, hashes of the prompt and context, tool, parameters, policy decision and approver. That one log serves security investigations, the AI Act's logging duties and the explanation rights in Colorado and California. Agent incidents get their own taxonomy, mapped to OWASP and MITRE ATLAS identifiers, because most will never have a CVE. Each agent also has named people with the authority to suspend it and to restore it.

Contracts with platform and tool vendors should cover incident notification and access to logs, advance notice when models or tool definitions change, and the information duties in Article 25(4) of the AI Act (AI Act Art. 25). Where negotiable, they should also extend indemnities beyond intellectual property. All of this should extend the organization's existing risk, release and incident processes rather than build a parallel bureaucracy, as Microsoft's operational guidance advises (Microsoft CAF). The whole set should sit inside an AI management system aligned with ISO 42001 and use the NIST AI RMF vocabulary.

Measure success with a scorecard that gates autonomy

Success metrics must answer one question for each process: has this agent earned more autonomy, the same autonomy, or less? BCG advises measuring "value, not adoption" (BCG). Microsoft tells teams to separate "measurable financial benefits from productivity improvements that don't directly affect financial results", and treats "cost per successful task" as a core measure, with "sustained cost increases or declining task success rates" triggering review (Microsoft CAF). ServiceNow's claimed $1 billion of "value" against about $200 million of actual savings shows why the distinction matters.

Dimension Metrics How the metric gates autonomy
Value Cost per case against the baseline measured before the agent; cycle time; revenue or retention effects; cash savings reported separately from time saved No expansion without value measured against the baseline
Quality Task success rate; pass^k across repeated runs; defect and rework rates; incidents by severity Promotion to L3 requires pass^k and incident thresholds that the business owner set in advance
Autonomy Share of cases handled end to end without a person (straight-through processing); escalation rate and escalation precision; rate at which people override or reverse the agent Rising overrides or reversals trigger review or demotion
Efficiency Cost per completed task, including human review and rework; tokens per task; 95th-percentile latency Sustained cost increases trigger review
Experience User satisfaction; how often users reject agent output; staff workload and strain Falling satisfaction blocks further rollout
Risk Policy violations; prompt-injection detections; permission drift; kill-switch activations and time to contain Any severe incident suspends autonomy until it is reviewed

No published benchmarks exist for straight-through processing or escalation rates by function, so the first quarter of each pilot sets the baselines that later targets are measured against. Escalation precision deserves special attention. It is the share of escalations that a human reviewer agrees needed escalating. An agent that escalates everything looks safe while delivering nothing, and an agent that never escalates looks efficient until it fails. Husain and Shankar recommend reporting results to stakeholders as a narrative with concrete examples rather than as a dashboard alone (Hamel Husain).

Conclusion

The evidence changes the question the team should ask. "How do we become an autonomous enterprise?" invites a platform purchase and a slogan. "Which of our processes has earned which level of delegated authority, and how would we know?" invites engineering. Seen that way, the 2026 data is less contradictory than it looks. Spending, protocol standards and identity tooling are racing ahead because they are prerequisites. Delegated authority lags because it has to be earned with evaluation data, incident history and proof that actions can be reversed, which most organizations have not yet produced. Autonomy and control are not opposites: the programs that will run the most autonomy in 2028 are the ones investing most now in the ability to take it back. The asset worth building is therefore not any single agent but the machinery for granting and revoking autonomy: eval suites built from real failures, an identity for every agent, a default-deny policy checkpoint, signed action logs and a scorecard. That machinery is also the part least exposed to vendor churn, as the short life of OpenAI's Agent Builder shows.

Three implications follow for the next 18 months. First, a 2027 shakeout will reward reversibility. The demotions and cancellations analysts predict will hit hardest the programs that granted autonomy uniformly, so being able to dial an agent down gracefully is a competitive advantage, not an admission of failure. Second, the evaluation engineer is the highest-leverage hire, because escalations and rework, not token prices, decide the unit economics. Third, the regulatory and technical calendars converge in 2027, and that rewards teams that build logging, human review, appeal routes and protocol migrations into the platform now rather than retrofitting them under deadline. The team can make the buzzword operational by publishing, each quarter, the share of its processes at each autonomy level and the evidence behind every promotion or demotion. Enterprises that look autonomous in 2028 will be the ones that granted autonomy process by process, on evidence, as telecom operators and trading desks did before them.
